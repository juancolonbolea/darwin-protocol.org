## What a proof is, here

A proof in this specification is a derivation, not an assertion. Each proof starts from a named set of axioms and previously-proved theorems, applies logical steps, and ends at a conclusion — marked $\blacksquare$ — that follows from the premises alone. No proof appeals to intuition, precedent, or authority. If a step doesn't follow from what came before it, the proof is broken, and so is everything built on top of it.

This is also why the dependency graph matters as much as any individual proof: a theorem is only as solid as the chain of axioms underneath it. Change an axiom, and every theorem that depends on it — directly or transitively — needs to be re-checked. That is the discipline the [Lean 4 formalisation](/specification/lean4/) is meant to enforce mechanically once it exists.

## The two empirical anchors: E1 and E2

Almost every claim in this specification derives from primitives, axioms, and other claims — a closed logical system. Two are anchored outside it. **E1 (T42, external validation)** ties the framework to reality outside itself. **E2 (A1, access dominance)** ties it to the problem it claims to solve. Both are measured rather than assumed.

### E1 — T42 (External Validation)

**T42 (External Validation)** is the claim that ties the framework's output to reality outside itself. The protocol's internal certification (the Quote) only becomes credible when overflow — the flow that doesn't match internally — beats an external benchmark. Internal matching rescues surplus; external overflow mints trust. Without that external check, internal certification is self-referential: the protocol grading its own students.

This makes T42 the framework's **falsifiability condition**. Every structural claim downstream — $e^X$ exponentiality (T5), aggregate α (T56), Open Trust's evolutionary attractor (T60), the routing-legitimacy argument the narrative synthesis rests on — ultimately depends on T42 holding empirically. If T42 fails persistently, the framework doesn't become false; it becomes a descriptive theory with no normative grip. We measured, we certified, we allocated — but the market disagreed.

That is not treated as a weakness in the design. A theoretical structure that cannot be disconfirmed has no grip on reality; T42 is what gives this one its grip. Its bilateral extension makes the anchor two-sided: external validation certifies both the minting of trust (overflow beats the benchmark) and its destruction (overflow underperforms) — a persistently negative T42 doesn't just mean the flywheel stopped; it means the meritocracy is destroying trust it previously minted. Operationally, T42 is not a proof filed once — it is measured continuously.

### E2 — A1 (Access Dominance and Cross-Domain Generality)

A1 asserts that where information asymmetry is structural and unevenly distributed, allocation converges on access rather than contribution, and that the advantage is self-reproducing. The convergence claim is the axiom. Its **magnitude** and its **generality across domains** are empirical, and E2 is where they are put at risk.

**Measurement.** In any market with observable allocation outcomes, regress realised allocation share on two things: a contribution estimator in A2's sense — ex-ante, out-of-sample, risk-adjusted — and an access proxy exogenous to contribution, such as latency tier, dealer or prime-broker relationship, a capital minimum met at entry, or a prior-standing flag. E2 claims three things about that specification: the standardised access coefficient is positive; it is not smaller than the standardised contribution coefficient; and it does not decay toward zero as the contribution estimator is improved. A fourth regression carries the self-reproduction leg — the access proxy at $t$ predicts the access proxy at $t+1$ more strongly than contribution at $t$ predicts it. Running the same specification in a second, structurally unrelated market — labour or capital allocation, not another trading venue — and recovering the same sign pattern is the cross-domain leg.

**Falsifier.** E2 fails where, in a market with structural information asymmetry and a competently specified contribution estimator, the access coefficient is statistically indistinguishable from zero, or is reliably below the contribution coefficient, or decays toward zero as estimator quality rises. It fails *as a general law* if that result appears in trading — the framework's first instance — or if the sign pattern does not replicate in the second domain.

**What a failure costs.** Unlike E1, a failed E2 breaks no derivation: the flywheel would still turn and the theorems would still follow. What it removes is the framework's *reason*. If access does not in fact dominate contribution, there is no asymmetry worth inverting, and the protocol is an elaborate answer to a problem that was not there. E1 is the framework's falsifiability condition; E2 is its motivating condition.

## Notation

The trust equation appears in different files under different variable names. This is the reconciliation, from `spec/CONSTITUTION.md` §IX.

| Framework symbol | Public / algebra symbol | Meaning | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| $C$ (P1) | $e$ | Credibility / edge rate | Stock (rate: $dC/dt$) |
| $K$ (P2), flow written $\Delta K$ | $\Delta C$ (confusingly) | Confidence / capital deployed | Flow → Stock (AuM) |
| $T = C \times K$ (P3) | $X$ | Trust = credibility × confidence | Stock (instantaneous product) |
| $dT/dt$ | $X(t+1) - X(t)$ | Trust accumulation rate | Flow (per period) |
| INDX $= \Delta \text{Quote} \times \text{Outside Confidence}$ | $e \times X$ | Trust earned per period | Flow (participation) |
| DWX | $e^X$ | Protocol ownership value (NPV of all future flows) | Stock |

**The apparent contradiction.** The framework states $T = C \times K$ — a product, trust as credibility times confidence. Elsewhere, trust stock is described by an accumulation equation: $X(t+1) = X(t) \times (1 + e) + \Delta C + N\tau$ — organic compounding of existing stock, plus new confidence inflows, plus subscription surplus.

These are not in conflict; they operate at different timescales. $T = C \times K$ is the *instantaneous minting rate* — how much trust is being produced right now. $X(t+1) = X(t)(1+e) + \Delta C + N\tau$ is the *stock accumulation equation* — how total trust stock evolves period over period. One is a flow equation; the other is periodic.

**A warning worth repeating:** the letter $C$ means *credibility* in the framework's own notation and *confidence flow* in the algebra convention. Which one is meant is determined by context, not by the letter alone. Because of that collision, the specification writes the confidence flow as $\Delta K$ — in A7, in Proof 1, and in Proof 19 — and reserves $\Delta C$ for quotations of the algebra convention.

See also: [The Math Behind Your Earnings](/about/algebra/) for the plain-language version of $e \times X$ and $e^X$.
